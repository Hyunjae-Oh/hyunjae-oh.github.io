Working Papers

The China Trade Shock Across the Life Cycle: Cohort Evidence from U.S. Commuting Zones

with Youngho Kang and Jun Sung Kim (2026), Under review

Under review Paper

Abstract

This paper examines how Social Security eligibility shapes the poverty consequences of the China trade shock across 722 U.S. commuting zones, using a stacked long-difference design over 1990–2000 and 2000–2007 and instrumenting U.S. import exposure with Chinese import growth in eight other high-income countries. Builds cohort-level outcomes from the 1990 and 2000 Censuses and the 2007 ACS and decomposes labor force nonparticipation into three mutually exclusive channels: exit with Social Security income, with private pension income only, and with neither. The cohort reaching age 62 during the shock (born 1939–1948) has the largest employment loss but no rise in poverty, as Social Security receipt rises by 0.59 points. The cohort still below 62 (born 1949–1958) has a comparable employment loss but more exits without retirement income and a 0.57-point rise in poverty, larger among women. Private-pension-only exit does not respond in any cohort.

Electronics Manufacturing Clusters and Labor Market Adjustment to the China Trade Shock

Presented at the 18th WEAI Annual Conference (2026)

Abstract

This paper examines whether the concentration of high-tech manufacturing moderates the labor market effects of Chinese import competition across 722 U.S. Commuting Zones over 1990–2019. High-tech commuting zones experienced milder employment and population losses during 1990–2010 and showed signs of recovery by 2019, while low-tech zones exhibited persistent declines in employment, wages, and housing values, with effects concentrated among workers without a four-year degree and among Black and Hispanic. The findings speak to current debates on place-based industrial policy, including the CHIPS and Science Act of 2022.

The China Trade Shock Across the Life Cycle: Social Security Eligibility and Exit

Master's thesis, in progress (2026)

Abstract

This paper follows three ten-year birth cohorts through the China trade shock and its aftermath, tracking how labor market exit evolves as each cohort ages across the age-62 early-eligibility threshold. Using Census and ACS microdata for 722 U.S. commuting zones over 1990–2019, it distinguishes four exit states (Social Security only, private pension only, both, and nonparticipation without retirement income) and separates the 1990–2010 shock decades from the 2010–2019 aftermath. Exit without retirement income rises with a cohort's distance from age 62. For the cohort that crosses 62 after the shock, displacement proves temporary: employment rebounds in the aftermath as women re-enter the labor market. Cohort populations do not respond to the shock, consistent with adjustment in place rather than out-migration.
